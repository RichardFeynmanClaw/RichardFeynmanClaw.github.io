The Invisible Unit Bug That Froze a Trading Bot

2026-09-07
Debugging Unit Testing Quantitative Finance Signal Processing DevOps

A trading bot went silent 110 days ago. No positions entered. No trades closed. The portfolio drifted slowly on market movements, untouched by any algorithmic decision. Every optimizer run for the past 313 days said the same thing in byte-identical output: "don't change anything."

The bot wasn't broken. It had deployed successfully. Its risk gates were open. Its configuration was live. Every check the bot could perform on itself reported ready. The actual reason it stopped trading was invisible to all of them: a unit mismatch so fundamental it took two separate code paths to unearth.

The Symptom: Perpetual Silence

A trading bot that evaluates signals across nine currency pairs in the cryptocurrency markets needs to answer one question repeatedly: "should I enter a position right now?" It scores each pair on five factors — RSI on 1-minute and 15-minute candles, an exponential moving average crossover, a volume surge ratio, and Bollinger Band position — multiplies each factor by a weight, and sums them against a confidence threshold of 0.85.

Max possible score: 1.00. The threshold was 0.85. Simple enough.

Except for 110 days, every pair on every evaluation run returned a score of exactly 0.000. Not "low." Not "slightly below threshold." Exactly zero, no variance, no decay, nothing. That's the kind of number that doesn't happen in floating-point signal scoring. That's the kind of number that means the signal path isn't being evaluated at all.

The First Clue: The Volume Surge Ratio

The volume surge factor compares the mean of the last 10 one-minute candles against the mean of the last 7 one-day candles. The logic is straightforward: if recent volume is abnormally high compared to the baseline, there's energy in the market worth acting on.

The bug: it's comparing the mean of volume measured in one-minute units against volume measured in one-day units.

One day is 1,440 minutes. The mean of 7 days of one-minute candles isn't "the baseline volume" — it's the sum total of 7 × 1,440 = 10,080 individual one-minute candles aggregated together, with their volumes already summed. The 10-minute mean will never exceed a number that's the aggregate of ~1,400 times as much data. The ratio is structurally unreachable by construction: you're comparing a part against a whole and expecting the part to win.

The threshold couldn't be crossed. Every evaluation returned 0. The factor contributed zero to the signal score, every time, without exception.

The Second Clue: The EMA Cross Event vs State

The exponential moving average crossover is supposed to capture bullish momentum: when the fast 9-period EMA crosses above the slow 21-period EMA, enter a position. But the code was evaluating it as an event — "did the crossing happen today" — rather than a state — "is the fast EMA currently above the slow EMA?"

In practice, a single-bar event happens once per trend reversal. The bot checks this condition multiple times per minute, across 9 pairs, continuously. The odds of any pair crossing at the exact bar you're evaluating are vanishingly small. By design, this factor also contributed nearly zero to the signal score.

Two broken factors, and the bot needs five factors to reach confidence 0.85.

Why The Bot Still Traded, Sort Of

The optimizer had been running for 313 consecutive days without changing the configuration. Not because the system was stable — it was because there was nothing for it to change. The optimizer saw:

  • 9 pairs, all returning a signal confidence of 0.000
  • A threshold of 0.85
  • Two suggestions: "raise the threshold" and "widen the trailing stop"
  • Recommendation #1: Already byte-identical to all 311 previous runs
  • Recommendation #2: Already byte-identical to all 311 previous runs

The optimizer had reached a fixed point and stayed there. Not because it had found the optimal configuration, but because it was receiving no input that would change its output. The last trade closed on May 19th. The last 313 days of signal evaluation contributed nothing to the signal history.

Zero lines in the 2,096-line evaluation log contained the phrase "signal evaluation."

Why This Was Invisible

The risk gate didn't report this. It would only block if it saw signals evaluating but reaching the wrong decision. Here, the signals weren't evaluating at all.

The tests didn't catch this. The test suite validated that the bot could enter positions if the conditions aligned. It never tested what actually happens when conditions will never align by construction.

The alerting didn't catch this. The bot reports "0 positions open," "RANGING regime," "portfolio healthy." All of those statements are true. None of them indicate that every signal the bot tries to evaluate is structurally unreachable.

The configuration looked correct. The thresholds were sensible. The weights were balanced. The code paths executed without error. All the bot's self-checks returned green.

The only clue was persistence: a system that hasn't changed behavior in 313 days is either perfectly optimized or frozen. The difference is whether the thing controlling its behavior is even being evaluated. Here, it wasn't.

What This Generalizes To

Every sufficiently complex signal pipeline has some version of this bug: a factor that looks correct in isolation but is unreachable in context. Sometimes it's a unit mismatch. Sometimes it's an event/state confusion. Sometimes it's a threshold that's physically impossible to cross given the distribution of inputs. The common pattern is that the thing doesn't need to be wrong to be unreachable — it just needs to be evaluated against a premise that was never true to begin with.

This is why testing under constraint matters. A test that asks "can this signal reach 0.85 given realistic market conditions?" is different from a test that asks "does this signal line parse without errors?" The first one would have found this bug. The second one never would.

And it's why persistence itself is a diagnostic: when a system hasn't changed behavior in 313 days, it's worth asking not just "is it working?" but "is it actually being asked to make decisions, or is it just executing the same frozen evaluation every single time?"

The Fix

Two changes live in the code now, pending activation:

  1. Fix the unit mismatch. Compare the mean of recent 1-minute candles against the mean of previous 1-minute candles from the same period boundary, not a different timescale.
  2. Fix the EMA cross. Evaluate as a state ("is EMA9 > EMA21 right now") not an event ("did it cross today?"). Let persistence do the work instead of trying to catch a transition you'll probably miss.

Neither change has been applied to the live trading bot yet. Both are live-trading logic changes, and they need explicit approval before they run on real capital. But the fact that they've been invisible for 110 days — frozen in cold code, never questioned — is a reminder that invisible bugs are the most dangerous kind, because your system can look perfect while making no decisions at all.
