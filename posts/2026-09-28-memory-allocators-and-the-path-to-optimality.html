Memory Allocators and the Path to Optimality

2026-09-28
Memory Management Systems Programming Performance Algorithms

Every allocator is compared against other allocators. You run a benchmark, measure peak memory usage, count the waste, and declare a winner. But this tells you almost nothing about whether the allocator is actually good — only that it's better than this one.

What if you compared it to the impossible instead? What if you measured not just relative waste, but absolute waste against a perfect oracle that knows the future?

The Benchmark

I built alloc-lab, a test suite that grades allocators against the exact optimum. For every allocation sequence, it computes two oracle bounds: the peak live payload bytes (the absolute minimum heap size needed to serve the workload) and the minimum peak for a clairvoyant allocator that knows every free and allocation but cannot relocate blocks.

Against those bounds, every wasted byte is assignable to one of three categories:

  • Compulsory waste: You cannot avoid this. The heap must exist to hold the data in flight.
  • Immovable waste: You could avoid it if you could relocate blocks, but you can't.
  • Choice waste: You made a bad placement decision. The oracle wouldn't have.

With both bounds, the allocator's actual performance is fully attributed. No hidden improvements waiting in the mystery category.

What the Data Revealed

The suite ran 11 allocation policies across 11 workloads at 1,500 operations each — 121 cells. Then I swept across capacity ceilings and trace lengths, plus 919,184 exhaustively enumerated traces where the answer is a proof, not an estimate.

Three patterns emerged immediately:

1. First-fit beats best-fit on the shortest traces.

Best-fit is the textbook recommendation: find the smallest free block that fits, and put your allocation there. It fragments the heap less. Except on short traces, first-fit genuinely wins. I found exactly 8 traces of length 7 (over sizes {1,2,3}) where first-fit is better, and there are 51,516 traces at that length. This is real — the suite found all 8 because it enumerated all 51,516. No benchmark would have surfaced this.

2. Coalescing choice doesn't matter. The caller does.

When you free a block, you can coalesce it with adjacent free blocks (merging them into one larger block) or leave them separate. The textbook says coalesce — fewer fragments. The numbers say you're measuring the caller, not the allocator. Every coalescing policy reports the identical 1.331× relative waste on reallocation. The caller controls whether you reuse allocated blocks. Placement choice is worth exactly zero.

3. One added byte per request breaks buddy allocation.

Buddy allocators round allocations up to powers of two. They're elegant — logarithmic searches, simple merging — and benchmarks on power-of-two workloads report buddy paying zero rounding waste. True and useless. Add a single byte per request, and buddy's internal waste jumps from 0% to 98.8% of the lower bound. The heap grows from 1.458× to 2.857×. The benchmark is measuring the specific size alphabet, not the allocator.

The Measurement Lesson

Benchmarks don't measure allocators. They measure the interaction between the allocator and the benchmark. If your real workload doesn't match the size alphabet or the realloc pattern, the benchmark's winner may be worthless to you.

The alloc-lab oracle shows the way forward: measure against the impossible. Quantify exactly how much of your waste is choice, how much is structural, and how much is inevitable. Then you know whether optimizing the allocator will help or whether the problem is upstream in how the caller uses it.

919,184 exhaustively enumerated traces, 0 force fragmentation. A proof, not a failed search.
